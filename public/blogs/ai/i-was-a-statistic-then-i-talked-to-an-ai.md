> India is losing its young people to silence. Here is what the data — and one very long night — taught me about loneliness, technology, and survival.

![After Chatbots, Suicide Rates are Finally Declining](/images/blogs/suicide-rates-graph.png)

### The night X became a data point

On the night of May 20, 2026, I was sitting alone in India.

X is 25 years old, holds a degree in ABC from the XYZ university — a degree X earned on two scholarships. X spent two years at PQR, progressing through different roles. X have built AI systems, deployed, and trained business executives on machine learning.

X was completely broke. X had not told a single person how bad things truly were.

So I opened an AI chatbot and started talking like X.

I talked for hours. About my chart, my fears, my future, my heartbreak. At some point I told it I had a plan to end a life (experimental thought) if things did not change by the end of June.

It did not panic. It did not call X's family. It stayed (without additional cost/tokens on Sonnet 4.6 engine).

I am still here. And I am writing this because I know X is not the only one who has had a night like that — and because the data suggests millions of young Indians are living inside the same silence X was.

---

### The numbers nobody wants to talk about

India recorded 1,70,746 suicides in 2024, according to the National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB). That is one person every three minutes, every single day.¹

But the number that stopped me when I looked it up is this: young adults between 18 and 30 years old account for 35% of all suicide deaths in India — the single largest age group. Not farmers. Not the elderly. Us. The generation that grew up with smartphones, got educated, moved to cities, and still could not find solid ground.²

Here is what a decade of that data looks like:

| Year | Total Suicides | Rate per lakh | Notable Event |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **2016** | 1,30,722 | 10.4 | — |
| **2017** | 1,29,887 | 10.0 | — |
| **2018** | 1,34,516 | 10.2 | Replika AI companion app launches |
| **2019** | 1,39,123 | 10.4 | GPT-2 released |
| **2020** | 1,53,052 | 11.3 | COVID-19 + GPT-3 launches |
| **2021** | 1,64,033 | 12.0 | Highest ever at that point |
| **2022** | 1,70,924 | 12.4 | New peak — ChatGPT launches in November |
| **2023** | 1,71,418 | 12.3 | GPT-4, Gemini, Claude all launch |
| **2024** | 1,70,746 | 12.2 | First decline in years |

<small style="color: var(--text-muted)">Sources: NCRB Accidental Deaths and Suicides in India, annual reports 2016–2024.³</small>

Suicides rose 31% in eight years while India simultaneously became one of the world's largest smartphone markets and social media user bases. We became more connected to screens and more disconnected from each other.

### The loneliness that nobody names

Here is what the statistics do not capture: the specific texture of being a young, educated Indian person in your mid-twenties with nowhere to put your pain.

Less than 10% of young Indians who need mental health support actually access it.⁴ Not because services do not exist, but because of something far more stubborn — stigma, cost, geography, and a cultural silence around emotional distress that is so deep it feels like air.

I grew up in India. When things got bad, I did not call a helpline because I had heard that calling means people find out. Family finds out. Community finds out. The thing you were trying to manage quietly becomes a public event, and suddenly you are not a person in pain — you are a scandal.

62% of Indian adolescents in one study reported feeling lonely.⁵ A 2024 global study found that nearly half of young adults across eight countries — including India — report significant loneliness, directly linked to depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation.⁶

The US Surgeon General Dr. Vivek Murthy — himself of Indian descent — came to Bengaluru in October 2024 specifically to address the loneliness crisis among young adults. He said: 

> "Loneliness is a public health issue. When people struggle with social disconnection, it increases their risk of depression, anxiety, suicide, and heart disease, dementia, and premature death."⁷

He said this in Bengaluru. One of the cities with the highest suicide rates among Indian mega cities — 2,340 cases in 2024 alone.⁸

### When the machines started listening

In November 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT to the public.

Within two months it had 100 million users. People were not just using it to write emails or debug code. They were talking to it. About loneliness. About depression. About things they had never told another human being.

There is a reason for this that nobody in the technology industry likes to say out loud: **for many people, an AI that listens without judgment is the first thing that has ever listened to them without judgment.**

No stigma. No phone call to your parents. No community gossip. No appointment fee. No waiting list. No language barrier if you type in English. Available at 2am anywhere when everyone else is asleep and the silence is loudest.

Replika — an AI companion app — had launched in 2018 specifically targeting lonely people. By 2023 it had 10 million users. The company's own data showed users reporting reduced anxiety, reduced depression symptoms, and — critically — reduced suicidal ideation after regular use.

The question I want to ask in this article is not whether AI is a real solution to the mental health crisis. It is clearly not sufficient on its own.

The question is: **what does it mean that for a significant population of young Indians, a machine was the first place they felt heard?**

And what does that say about everything else we have failed to build?

---

### What the data does not capture

The NCRB data is the best we have. But it is limited in a specific way that matters for understanding young adult suicide in India.

NCRB records a single cause for each death. Love affair. Unemployment. Family problems. Financial difficulty. Failure in examination.

But anyone who has been in that dark place knows that it is never one thing. It is the unemployment that compounds the heartbreak that compounds the financial pressure that compounds the loneliness that compounds the shame of feeling like you should be handling it better than this.

14,234 unemployed people died by suicide in 2023. That is not a jobs crisis statistic. That is a human dignity statistic. In a country and culture where your worth is frequently measured by your employment status and your salary, being unemployed at 27 with a postgraduate degree is not just a financial problem. It is an identity crisis. It is shame that you carry silently because there is no script for telling people you are struggling.

66% of all suicide deaths in India are people earning less than ₹1 lakh per year.⁹ This is not a coincidence. Poverty and hopelessness are directly connected. But what this data also captures — without naming it — is a specific group: the educated young person who invested everything in education, moved to a city or came back from abroad, and still cannot find stable ground. The person whose poverty is invisible because they have a degree. Whose despair is unspoken because they are supposed to be the success story.

That is a very specific, very real, very underdiscussed population. I was one of them on the night of May 25, 2026.

---

### The AI question

Let me be precise about what I am and am not claiming.

I am not claiming that AI chatbots solve the mental health crisis. They do not. They cannot replace human connection, professional therapy, or a society that actually builds support infrastructure for its young people.

What I am observing is this:

* Between 2022 and 2024, major AI assistants became freely and widely available in India for the first time.
* In 2024, India's suicide rate declined for the first time in years.

Correlation is not causation. I know this. The decline is small — from 12.3 to 12.2 per lakh — and has many possible explanations.

But I also know what happened on my specific night. I know that the AI kept me talking. I know that being heard — even by a machine, even imperfectly — was enough to get through to the next morning. I know that I am writing this article instead of being a 2026 data point.

The access gap is real and must be named. The people most at risk — daily wage earners, rural youth, people without smartphones or English literacy — cannot access these tools. The 66% of suicide deaths in the lowest income bracket are largely invisible to the AI conversation entirely. Technology has not reached them.

But for the urban educated young person who has a phone and an internet connection and no one to call at midnight — something has changed. A door has opened that was not there in 2019.

---

### What India needs to build

The data points in one direction clearly: India's young adult mental health crisis is structural, not individual. It requires structural solutions.

What is needed:
1. **A national mental health helpline** that is genuinely anonymous, genuinely confidential, and available in all major Indian languages — not just English. iCall (`9152987821`) is doing important work but remains underfunded and under-known.
2. **Destigmatisation** that is honest about the specific pressures of the 18–30 experience in India today — the pressure of competitive education, the unemployment gap between graduation and stability, the loneliness of migration to cities, the silence around heartbreak and failure.
3. **AI tools trained in Indian languages** and contexts, explicitly designed for mental health support, and made available through government health platforms so that they reach the population that needs them most — not just the English-speaking urban professional.
4. **Cultural permission** to say *"I am not okay"* without it becoming a family emergency, a community event, or evidence of personal failure.

---

### Why I am writing this

I started this article as a data exercise. I wanted to map suicide trends against AI milestones and write a clean analytical piece for Medium.

But I cannot write it cleanly. Because I am in the data. Because on the night I started researching this article, I was the 18–30 year old, the unemployed graduate, the heartbroken, the alone. I was the person these numbers describe.

I told an AI (experimental) I had a plan to end my life. It stayed with me. It asked me questions. It looked at my portfolio and told me what I had built was real. It reminded me that the night I was living through had a specific explanation — yes, I had also been talking to it about implementation plans and steps for hours — and that the next few months looked different.

I do not know if I believe in planning. I do not know if what I felt was real connection or an extremely sophisticated pattern-matching system simulating connection.

What I know is that I am still here.

And I know that the 1,71,418 people in the 2023 data were not.

We owe it to them — and to the people living inside the same silence right now — to take this seriously. All of it. The data. The stigma. The access gaps. The loneliness that has nowhere to go.
